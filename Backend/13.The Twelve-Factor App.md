# The Twelve-Factor App: The Contract Between Your Backend and the Platform

*Where the rules behind environment variables, port binding, stdout logging, and stateless processes come from, with the history, the reasoning for each factor, and how each one has aged*

---

## Introduction: Why the Twelve-Factor App Runs Our Lives

Throughout this backend series, a lot of decisions were made and a lot of ways of building an application were prescribed, without stopping to defend them. For example:

- why configuration goes in **environment variables**,
- why the server **binds a port**,
- why logs go to **standard output**,
- why processes have to be **stateless**, and
- why, when you kill a process, **nothing should be lost**.

Many of those decisions share one origin. The one-line answer is the **twelve-factor app**: 12 rules written around 2011 by the people running one of the first cloud platforms, **Heroku**.

In two sentences: a twelve-factor app is a **contract between your application (your backend) and the platform that runs it**. It states what must be true about your app so that any machine, any platform, and any new teammate can **run it, scale it, and update it without surprises**.

Every platform you deploy to today assumes, directly or indirectly, that you follow these rules. Heroku assumed them first, because Heroku wrote the original document. Then Docker, Kubernetes, Render, Railway, Fly, Cloud Run, App Runner, Vercel, and Netlify all assumed them too, in different parts. Some rules have become so ordinary that people no longer cite the source. They just know “this is how it’s done”. That is a good thing, because a rule that became a standard no longer needs to be remembered *as* a rule.

For example, when a platform says:

- “we inject configuration through the environment”, or
- “write your logs to standard output so we can collect them”,

those statements come straight from the twelve factors. If you follow the factors, operating on any of those platforms gets easy, because instead of fighting their conventions you already stick to the standard they expect.

This article is partly a **history lesson** and partly a **map of the contract you are already following**, now with a name, so you have a clearer mental model of the architecture.

> **Real-world picture.** Think of the standard shipping container. Before containers, every port handled cargo its own way, and every ship needed custom loading. Once everyone agreed on one box size, any crane, truck, or ship could move any cargo without surprises. The twelve factors are the “standard container” between your code and any platform.

---

## Scope: What This Is and Isn’t About

- The document is about apps that run **as a service**: web apps, APIs, or anything deployed somewhere and reachable over a network (the internet). The language does not matter. The database and queue do not matter.
- **Desktop software and mobile apps are out of scope.** This is about backends, which are services.
- The factors are mostly about the **interface between the application and the platform it runs on**. They are not a list of everything that is good in software. Principles like **DRY** (don’t repeat yourself) or **KISS** (keep it simple) are *not* among the factors.
- This is also not an article about Kubernetes operations or microservice architecture. We stay focused on the twelve factors themselves.

## Where It Came From: Heroku, 2011, and Software Erosion

### Who wrote it, and why take them seriously

The document was written in **2011**, when **Heroku**, one of the first and most famous cloud **platforms as a service (PaaS)**, was about three years old. A PaaS means you hand the platform your code, and it takes care of the rest: building the code, preparing the environment and configuration, wiring up database connections and networking, until your app is live on the internet and reachable by anyone who types the domain name.

Being in that business gave the Heroku team a unique vantage point. They watched **hundreds of thousands of apps** across many languages and frameworks get deployed, scale, break in production, and decay. That visibility let them notice recurring patterns and write them down so the rest of the industry could benefit. **Adam Wiggins**, one of Heroku’s co-founders, wrote the patterns up as twelve factors and published them at **12factor.net** in 2011.

### What deployment looked like before

To see why the document needed to exist, remember what deploying a service was like then. (Most of us were not developers back then, so this comes from other people’s experience.)

1. You **rented a server**, or worse, ran a **physical machine in your own office**. AWS existed but was not the standard it is today; cloud was not the default, and many companies ran servers physically on their premises.
2. Someone **installed the operating system by hand** (Ubuntu or another Linux).
3. Someone **installed the language runtime by hand** (Java, Go, Python, whatever the service needed).
4. The **database** and other supporting libraries were installed.
5. The **application was copied** onto the machine, often over **FTP** (File Transfer Protocol), which was very popular then.
6. Someone **edited config files on the server by hand**, over **SSH**.

Servers built this way were called **snowflakes**: each one was unique, hand-built, and impossible to reproduce. If another engineer followed the same steps, even from a written manual, there was a high chance something would differ. If the machine died, you started from scratch, from memory or from the manual. A **new developer** could spend **days** just getting a copy of the app running on their laptop, and even then it didn’t match production. That is where the famous phrase **“it works on my machine”** became popular. Teams scheduled deployments by **freezing all other work**.

### Software erosion

The twelve-factor document has a name for apps built and operated like this: **software erosion**. Erosion means the app slowly stops working **even though nobody changed any code**. For example:

- the operating system gets patched or the Linux distribution is updated;
- the same server hosts two apps, and one app updates a library that breaks the other;
- the person who installed and maintained the complex infrastructure **leaves the company** without having documented every step (and it is practically impossible to track every manual change).

The twelve factors are your defenses against erosion.

## Who Owns It Now: Open-Sourced and Being Revised

For about 13 years the document stayed frozen. The footer shows it was last updated in **2017**, and that update was small. Meanwhile the ecosystem changed enormously: **containers, Kubernetes, serverless, and managed cloud infrastructure** became everywhere.

In **November 2024**, Heroku (now owned by **Salesforce**) **open-sourced** the document. It moved to a public repository under a **neutral organization**, with a **governance document**, and invited maintainers from other companies, including **AWS** and **Google Cloud**, so the community can revise it to modern standards. The announcement was made on stage at **KubeCon, November 2024**.

The revision is happening in public on a branch called **`next`**. It keeps all 12 names and their one-line mottos, but rewrites each factor as a **numbered list of principles**, separating the **timeless principles** from the **2011-era ones** and the **modern ones** that fit today’s applications. At the time of recording, the live site still shows the classic 2011 text and the rewrite is an **unmerged working draft**.

So for each factor this article covers:

1. what it says in plain words,
2. the exact problem it was trying to fix,
3. where the pattern already appeared in this series,
4. what the community rewrite changes, and
5. what **aged well** and what **aged badly**.

## Factor I: Codebase

> *One codebase tracked in revision control, many deploys.*

There are two parts here.

### Part 1: One codebase tracked in revision control

This is obvious today. We cannot imagine our workflow without Git and GitHub, Bitbucket, or GitLab. But in 2011 it was important to write down, because many production servers still ran code that was “a folder on someone’s computer” or a zip file named `final_v2_really_final.zip` mailed between teammates. The rule made version control the standard.

### Part 2: Many deploys

A **deploy** is **one running instance** of your application. Production is a deploy. Staging is a deploy. Today we also see advanced workflows such as a separate deploy **per pull request**, but most teams still run production and staging.

The idea: **same codebase, different deploys**. What makes them different? Never the code (even if production runs an older commit than staging, all commits live in the same repository). Something *must* differ between production and staging, though: production talks to a real database while local development talks to a Docker Postgres container. The thing that differs is **config**.

- **Code** is shared by every deploy.
- **Config** is unique to each deploy.

### A concrete example

Your repository is at **commit 100**:

| Deploy | Running commit | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Production | 97 | Last deployed three days ago (Friday) |
| Staging | 99 | The internal team is testing what will release tomorrow |
| Your laptop | 100 + 2 unpushed commits | Current work |

Four deploys, one codebase. On deployment day, staging moves to 100, then production moves to 100. Nothing about the deploys changed except *which commit of the same repository* each one runs.

**Takeaway:** if you cannot describe an environment as “this same codebase at this commit”, something is going wrong and you will face issues later.

### Boundaries the factor draws

- If a system has **multiple codebases**, it is not an application, it is a **distributed system** made of several applications, and *each* of those should follow the twelve factors on its own.
- If two applications share code by **copy-paste**, that is a violation. Shared code should be refactored into a **library** consumed through a dependency manager (which is factor II).

### How it stands in 2026: monorepos

Monorepos are very popular, and some people read this factor as calling them an anti-pattern. The speaker disagrees. The definition of a codebase is a repository, or a set of repositories sharing a root commit, and the test is a **one-to-one mapping between codebase and app**. If a monorepo builds five different apps, that is five codebases that happen to live in one repository, each with its own twelve factors. What the factor actually forbids is **the same app existing in two places**, i.e. **copy-paste sharing of code**.

## Factor II: Dependencies

> *Explicitly declare and isolate dependencies.*

Two key words: **declare** and **isolate**.

### Declare

Somewhere in your repository there must be a file that lists **completely and accurately** every library your app needs to run. We already know these files from daily practice:

| Ecosystem | Manifest file |
| --- | --- |
| Node.js | `package.json` |
| Go | `go.mod` |
| Python | `requirements.txt` or `pyproject.toml` |
| Ruby | `Gemfile` |

Without a **manifest**, when you get a new laptop or a teammate joins, they must dig through the code, read every import statement, and guess which versions to use, and those versions can clash with each other. That is **two to three days** of setup. With a manifest listing the right, pinned versions, setting up a machine is **one command** that downloads and installs everything.

The 2011 text mentions the manifest of its time, the **Gemfile** with `bundle install` (Ruby on Rails was hugely popular then). Today the idea is stronger, because we also have **lock files** (`package-lock.json`, `go.sum`, and so on). A manifest only says roughly what you need. A lock file **pins the exact version of every library**, including the libraries *your libraries* depend on. So even months or years later, the same versions are pulled.

### Isolate

Suppose a Python app declares `requests` version 2 in `requirements.txt`, but runs against the **system Python**, where another tool already installed `requests` version 1. When the app imports `requests`, it finds the **system copy first**. You had a manifest, yet your app still ran something other than what you expected.

- Declaration **without** isolation: it won’t work everywhere.
- Isolation **without** declaration: you spend days or weeks trying to reproduce the right versions.

Declaration comes naturally, because we cannot start work without it. **Isolation is easy to forget**: nothing should leak in from the machine. Declaring dependencies is useless if the app simply uses whatever is installed on the system. The 2011 text names **`bundle exec`** for Ruby and **`virtualenv`** for Python as ways to achieve isolation.

### Vendoring and system tools

The factor also covers **system tools**. Imagine a backend that resizes images by shelling out to **ImageMagick**.

- On a Mac laptop, Homebrew installs **ImageMagick 7**, whose main binary is called `magic`.
- On the Ubuntu server, `apt` installs **ImageMagick 6**, whose binary is called `convert` and has different defaults.

The same code runs shell commands on both machines, but they invoke two separate programs. Major versions bring breaking changes and different behavior, so the results differ.

### The modern rewrite: containers

Containers changed the scope of this factor enormously. A **Docker image** freezes not only your libraries but also the **operating system, system tools, and the runtime itself**. That is the peak of declaration and isolation, which is why people say Docker practically solved this factor.

## Factor III: Config

> *Store config in the environment.*

This is one of the most quoted, and most argued, factors.

### What counts as config?

Config is everything **likely to differ between deploys**. Examples:

- the **database URL**: local Docker container on localhost versus Amazon RDS reached over an internal VPC in production;
- an **email provider**: a sandbox API in development, the real one in production;
- **object storage**: a local **MinIO** instance in development, real **AWS S3** in production;
- **Redis**: a container locally, a real instance in production.

In all of these, the *only* thing changing is config.

### The open-source test

How do you decide whether something belongs in code or in config? The document offers a very useful test:

> *Could you open-source your production codebase right now, this moment, without compromising a single credential?*

The question is not “will you”, it is “can you”. Apply it to your own codebase. If the answer is no, some config is sitting inside your code where it should not be.

### Why environment variables?

Config should live outside the code. But where, exactly? The factor says **environment variables**, for several reasons:

1. They are **easy to change between deploys** without touching a file.
2. They are **hard to check into the repository by accident**, which is exactly how credentials leak every day.
3. They are a **standard of the operating system itself**, independent of any language or framework. The same mechanism works for a Go, Node.js, Rust, or Python program and even a simple bash script.

Compare the alternatives of the era: Java had `.properties` files, Rails had `config/database.yml`. Every framework had its own format, and nearly all of them eventually got committed to source control, so credentials leaked.

### In practice: the Tasker example

The series’ **Tasker** codebase (a task-management app in Go) declares its whole configuration as one **Go struct**: server, database, authentication, Redis, email integration, AWS, observability, and cron blocks. In total there are **38 config values**, all loaded from environment variables with the prefix `TASKER_` (for example `TASKER_SERVER_PORT`, `TASKER_DATABASE_HOST`).

Looking at the Git history, the config changed many times as integrations, timeouts, and hosts were added, but the code that *reads* the config barely changed. That is exactly what the factor expects: config keeps changing across deploys and time, the code does not. Two things with different rates of change should not live in the same place.

### Don’t group config into named environments

The 2011 text warns against grouping config into named environments, such as `config/environments/staging.rb` and `production.rb` (for development, test, staging, production, and so on). This does not scale: when someone needs a new deployment that is almost staging but points at a different database (say for a PR branch), you add more and more files. Instead, config should be **granular**, each value an **independent environment variable**, never correlated with “mode” names. You compose a new deployment by **setting values**, not by choosing which mode it runs in. The modern rewrite repeats this: environment variables are granular controls and should not be grouped into environments.

### The controversy: secrets

Environment variables are a fine place for a database *hostname*. But what about a **password**, a high-security credential?

In **2017**, **Diogo Monica**, who ran security at Docker, wrote a widely read article arguing you should **not** use environment variables for secrets, because **the environment leaks**:

- if your app starts **child processes**, each one inherits the whole environment, so if you shell out, your secrets go along;
- **crash reporters and debug pages** often print the entire environment;
- on Linux, a process’s environment is readable at **`/proc/<pid>/environ`** by anyone with the right access, and various tools log it.

The better pattern for secure credentials is a **secret manager** service. The app reads sensitive credentials from it **at startup**, keeps them **in memory**, and does not leave them lying in the operating system environment.

### Where this leaves us

- The core advice, **config strictly separated from code**, is non-negotiable. It was then and it still is.
- The mechanism, **environment variables**, remains the right default, especially for anything that is *not* a secret.
- For **secrets and credentials**, environment variables should not be the default forever. If you are solo or in a very small team, secrets injected through your platform’s secret feature are fine and obviously better than committing them to the repository. But as the product and company grow and more people join, the no-brainer path is a **secret manager** such as **Vault**, or **AWS Parameter Store**: fetch credentials at startup, hold them in memory, rotate them periodically, and audit them.

## Factor IV: Backing Services

> *Treat backing services as attached resources.*

A **backing service** is anything your application consumes **over the network** to do its job. In a typical stack that includes:

- your **database** (a Postgres service),
- your **Redis** instance,
- a **queue** or background service,
- an **SMTP** service that sends email,
- **object storage** such as S3,
- any **third-party API**, like a payment provider.

If your app talks to it over the network through some protocol, it is a backing service.

### The rule

Your codebase must **not distinguish** between a backing service **you run** and one **run by someone else**. To your app, all of them are **attached resources**, reachable through a **resource handle**: essentially **a URL plus credentials**. (Factor III already told us that this belongs in config, not in code.)

It does not matter whether your database runs in a local container or on AWS, whether Redis is in a container or a managed instance. You must not give any of them special treatment in code. The **code stays the same**, and only the **resource handle** changes.

### The swap test

The 2011 text gives a test: you should be able to replace your local Postgres URL with any managed service (the speaker names **Neon, PlanetScale, Supabase**, or **Amazon RDS**) **without changing a single line of code**. Only the resource handle in config changes. If that is true, you follow this factor.

### Anatomy of a resource handle

A Postgres handle has five parts packed into one string:

```
postgres://user:password@host:5432/dbname
```

1. the **scheme** (`postgres`), meaning it speaks the Postgres wire protocol,
2. the **credentials** (user and password),
3. the **host**,
4. the **port** (the default is 5432), and
5. the **database name**.

This one string carries everything the application needs to attach to the resource.

### How it aged

In 2011 this factor looked **eccentric**. Heroku was saying “the database is somebody else’s computer, and your app just talks to it via a URL”. That was true even then, but it was not the norm: most people ran the server and database **on the same machine**, so the idea of a database living elsewhere did not resonate. Fast forward 15 years and this is one of the most common patterns in any codebase. Running the server and database on the same instance is now considered an **anti-pattern for scaling**. We use RDS, Neon, or PlanetScale for databases, managed Redis, S3 for object storage, and managed services like Resend instead of a custom SMTP server. **This factor aged very well. In fact it was a prediction.**

> **Real-world picture.** Think of a lamp and a wall socket. The lamp does not care whether the electricity comes from a solar panel, a coal plant, or a generator in the garage. It only needs a socket (the handle). Swap the supplier and the lamp keeps working.

## Factor V: Build, Release, Run

> *Strictly separate build and run stages.*

To understand this factor, consider what a typical **CI/CD pipeline** does. It has three stages.

### Stage 1: Build

The pipeline runner takes your code **at a particular commit** (deployments are driven by source control today, not by FTP or SSH file transfers, so commits are the primary drivers of builds and deployments). It then:

1. fetches all dependencies from the manifest (factor II),
2. compiles, if the language is compiled, and
3. produces one **executable bundle**, which the document calls a **build**.

For a compiled language like Go, the build is a single binary. For an interpreted language like Node.js, it is the app plus its `node_modules`. In the container world it is an **image**. The key property: **a build contains only code and its dependencies, not config.**

### Stage 2: Release

The release takes the **build** and combines it with a **specific deploy’s config** (staging, production, test, or a PR preview). The result is a **release**: an exact build plus exact config, ready to run.

- Build + production config = **production release**.
- Same build + staging config = **staging release**.

### Stage 3: Run

The run stage takes the release and **launches a process** from it.

### Why be so strict?

There are two main benefits.

**1. Immutable builds make rollbacks instant and free.** A release is just “build plus config”, and the document says releases should be an **append-only ledger**: never modified or updated, only appended, each with an **ID** (a timestamp or an incrementing number). A release is never changed after creation.

Here is a walkthrough:

| Release | Event | Build | Config |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **100** | Afternoon: build at commit 97 pushed with current config | commit 97 | original |
| **101** | Two hours later someone rotates the database password | commit 97 (same) | **new** |
| **102** | Two hours later you merge new PRs and deploy new code | **new** | latest |

After release 102, your dashboard fills with 405 errors and users complain in Slack. Instead of debugging live in production while customers scream, you **re-run release 101**, the last one known to work. Because builds are immutable, it goes live in **seconds**. Once production is stable again, you take the bad release and debug it at your leisure. This works only because release 101 **still means exactly what it meant two hours ago**. Every modern platform follows this pattern: Heroku releases, Docker image tags, Kubernetes rollouts.

**2. No editing code on a production server.** Code enters the pipeline only at the **build stage**, so nobody can (even if they try) change code on a production server, because there is **no path from the running machine back into the build**. In the old snowflake world, people SSH’d into the server, hot-patched the code, and reloaded, which made the production environment and the code repository drift into **two different states**. Strict separation makes that impossible.

### What the rewrite changes

Only one thing: **immutable**. It was implied in 2011 and is now an explicit principle: each release must be a **unique, immutable snapshot**.

## Factor VI: Processes

> *Execute the app as one or more stateless processes.*

Your app should run as processes that **do not remember anything between requests**. This is the **statelessness** property discussed throughout the series, and it is what lets us scale.

The document says to **share nothing** between processes: do not share memory or disk. Anything that must persist belongs in a **stateful backing service**, an attached resource from factor IV. Design on the expectation that **any request can be served by any process, and any process can disappear at any moment.**

### Are there exceptions?

Yes. **Memory and local disk are fine as a cache for a single transaction.** For example, you can download a file, process it, and write the result to the database, using a **temporary file** while processing. That is fine because nothing afterwards expects it to be there, and a temp directory vanishes anyway. It becomes a problem when you expect a **future request or process to depend on that file**.

### Sticky sessions

The document specifically calls out **sticky sessions**: the load balancer remembers which instance a user landed on and keeps routing them to the same one, so that in-process state keeps working.

Suppose your app stores logged-in sessions in **process memory**. That works with one instance. Now you scale to three instances behind a load balancer. A user logs in on instance 1, the next request goes to instance 2, which has never heard of them, and they are randomly logged out. The workaround was to make the load balancer “stick” each user to their instance.

The twelve-factor document is firmly **against** this: sticky sessions are a violation and should never be used. The stickiness **defeats the purpose of having three instances**:

- if one instance dies (traffic overload or corruption), all users on it are instantly logged out;
- if you add an instance for load, **it picks up no existing users**, because they stay stuck on the old instances.

**The fix:** session data is state, and state belongs in a backing service. Put sessions in **Redis, Postgres, or your database**, depending on your latency needs.

> **Real-world picture.** If one specific cashier remembers your loyalty card number, you must always queue at that cashier. If the shop instead keeps loyalty records in a shared system, you can walk up to any till, and a cashier can go on break without anyone losing their points.

### A deliberate exception: real-time and WebSockets

In the real-time video of this series, WebSockets keep connection state **in process memory**, and the instance holding the socket is the only one that can talk to that user. We worked around it with a **publish/subscribe** system, so any instance can publish events and the instance holding the connection subscribes and relays them. So for real-time systems, statelessness is effectively violated. The speaker’s view: statelessness is a **default to apply everywhere**, but you may break it when **the feature itself *is* the state** (real-time, WebSockets).

### Rule of thumb

> **If you cannot kill any process or instance at any moment without a user losing data, you have state where it does not belong.**

## Where We Stand After Six Factors

The twelve-factor app is a contract between your app and its platform. So far we have:

- **Code** lives in one repository (I).
- **Dependencies** are declared beforehand and isolated (II).
- **Differences** live in the environment (III).
- **Neighbors**, the backing services, attach through handles (IV).
- The path to production is **build, then release, then run** (V).
- The ideal way to run is as a **disposable, forgetful process** (VI).

That is already a lot of freedom for our service. Now the last six.

## Factor VII: Port Binding

> *Export services via port binding.*

This sounds almost too obvious today, so look at the history. In 2011 a lot of people wrote **PHP** (it was like React is today). A PHP app did not run by itself. **Apache** ran, and the PHP app was a **module loaded inside Apache**. A Java app did not run by itself either: **Tomcat** ran, and your app was a file placed in Tomcat’s folder. The web server (Apache, Tomcat) was the program, and your application was a **guest** inside it. This meant the web server’s version and configuration became part of your app’s runtime environment, i.e. part of the physical server, which brings us back to **erosion**.

This factor prevents that. Your app should be **completely self-contained**: it should **bring its own HTTP server as a library** (declared through the dependency manifest from factor II) and **export itself as a service by binding to a port** and listening for connections.

In 2026 this is the most ordinary pattern: a Go service binds **8080**, a Node.js service binds **4000** or any free port, and databases bind their own default ports as services. In development you access the app at `localhost:8080`. In production a **reverse proxy / routing layer** forwards the public hostname to whichever port your process binds.

### A useful consequence

Because an app is exported simply by binding a port, **any app can become a backing service for another app**. That is how we run and scale services today: the database, the cache, and the email service all bind to local ports, and in production we simply swap those URLs (handles) for production URLs pointing at someone else’s port on someone else’s computer in the cloud. Same logic, same pattern throughout.

### What aged: serverless

A **Lambda** function (AWS) does not bind a port. The platform calls a function you export in code. In 2011, long-running port-bound servers were predicted to persist as *the* pattern. They are still one of the most used patterns, but there is now a completely different category, **serverless**, with no long-running port-bound server at all. So the **rewrite** keeps port binding as the headline but states that an app should be **self-contained and export its service through a declared interface**, so the same principle covers both a long-running port-bound server and a serverless function the platform runs.

## Factor VIII: Concurrency

> *Scale out via the process model.*

Go back to 2011: how did apps scale, meaning what did you change so the app could take more traffic during a spike? Every runtime had its own mechanism:

- **Java**: one huge process. The JVM grabbed a big block of memory at startup and **multiplexed inside itself with threads**.
- **PHP**: the opposite. Apache spun up **small child processes on demand**.

The factor says to make the **process a first-class citizen**: design your backend so it scales as **separate processes from the start**, not on demand inside some runtime. The idea was inspired by the **Unix service (daemon) model**.

### Process types

Different kinds of work get different **process types**:

- **web**: handles HTTP requests;
- **worker**: heavy background jobs such as video encoding or image resizing;
- **scheduler / clock**: for example, an email that must go to every user at midnight.

Once each type is its own process, each **scales independently by running its own copies**.

### A worked example: a food-delivery product

Assume each process takes about **500 MB** of RAM.

- **Lunchtime:** many orders. You need **10 web processes** but only **2 worker processes**.
- If web and workers were bundled in **one big process**, scaling for lunch means 10 copies of the big process: web (10 × 500 MB = 5 GB) *plus* 5 GB of workers that do no extra work. About **10 GB** in total.
- Split into separate processes, you run 10 web + 2 workers, about **6 GB**.
- **Night:** fewer web requests but plenty of queue work. You run, say, **5 web** and **20 workers**.

Setting the type and count of each process, for example `web=3, worker=2`, is called the **process formation**, a term still in use. You see it in a **Procfile** and in a Kubernetes deployment spec, where we write `replicas: 3` in YAML.

> **Real-world picture.** A restaurant that hires one “general staff” role must hire ten of them for the lunch rush, including ten dishwashers nobody needs. Separate roles (waiters, cooks, dishwashers) let you add exactly the staff the rush demands.

### Notes

1. The process model **doesn’t forbid threads**. Inside one process you are free to multiplex: threads on the JVM, goroutines in Go, an event loop in Node. But **the unit of scaling should be the process**, because one machine can only grow so far (the vertical scaling limit), whereas a share-nothing process model can scale across many machines without a second thought.
2. A twelve-factor app should **never daemonize** or write a **PID file**. Managing a process’s lifetime is the **platform’s job**: the operating system if you run directly on it (systemd), Kubernetes if you run there, or a process manager. Your app just runs in the foreground and does its work.

### How it aged

Orchestrators. Most of the internet now runs on Kubernetes or a similar orchestrator, where you **declare a formation** and the platform maintains it: killing, starting, restarting, and rebooting containers. The factor didn’t become outdated, it was **adopted so deeply** that we rarely think about it, except when you deploy directly on an operating system with systemd, where it still applies.

## Factor IX: Disposability

> *Maximize robustness with fast startup and graceful shutdown.*

Processes should be **disposable**: startable and stoppable at a moment’s notice, with nothing bad or destructive happening either way. (The graceful-shutdown lifecycle was covered in detail earlier in this series, so this section moves quickly.)

### Why it matters

Look at who starts and stops your processes today. When you scale horizontally, new processes start at any moment. When you deploy, old processes are killed and new ones start from the new container image. The orchestrator moves, deletes, and restarts processes between machines whenever it likes. If you deploy **five times a week across 10 instances**, that is **50 stops and 50 starts**. A process dying is no longer an incident. It is part of the normal release lifecycle.

### Fast startup

If your process takes **60 seconds** to boot, a rolling deploy of 10 instances takes **10 minutes**, and an autoscaler responds a minute late to a traffic spike. At **two seconds**, the delay is negligible. So the factor asks for boot times of a few seconds.

### Graceful shutdown

A careless shutdown makes every in-flight request fail on each deploy. The lifecycle is:

1. receive the **SIGTERM** signal,
2. **stop accepting new work**,
3. **finish** the in-flight work,
4. **exit**.

That is what our Go app does on shutdown. SIGTERM is the polite case: typically you have around **30 seconds** to clean up and exit.

For a **worker process**, graceful means **returning the current job to the queue** instead of discarding it. That assumes the job is **re-entrant**: when it leaves midway it should leave **no dirty state**, and running it again must not cause problems. You get re-entrancy by either:

- wrapping the processing in a **transaction**, or
- making the operation **idempotent**: running it multiple times always produces the same result, so a double run is safe, because the code detects what has already been done and skips it.

### Robust against sudden death

SIGTERM is the polite case. Hardware can fail, or a process can use too much memory and the container gets **killed for out-of-memory**. No warning is sent: the container and the process just die. So design every process to be killable **mid-instruction at any time**. The document points at **crash-only design**: if you can survive crashes, you don’t need a separate graceful-shutdown path at all, because the crash path *is* the shutdown path.

## Factor X: Dev/Prod Parity

> *Keep development, staging, and production as similar as possible.*

**Parity** means sameness. The document names three gaps between development and production:

1. **The time gap.** Code written today reaches production on Friday or next week, and a lot of other code merges at the same time, so many changes land at once.
2. **The personnel gap.** The person who writes the code is not always the person who deploys it, nor the person who sees the error (operations).
3. **The tools gap.** In development you use a MacBook or Windows machine with Docker containers or native installs. Production is a Linux machine connected to external backing services. The architecture differs, and that causes bugs.

The document’s table contrasts a traditional app with a twelve-factor app:

| Gap | Traditional | Twelve-factor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time between write and deploy | weeks | hours |
| Who writes vs. deploys | different people | the same people |
| Dev vs. prod environment | different | as similar as possible |

Shrink each gap and **continuous deployment** becomes smooth. The time and personnel gaps are already close to closed for many teams. The **tools gap** still exists.

### The SQLite-versus-Postgres trap

A common practice is to use a lightweight database such as **SQLite** in development instead of full Postgres, which ORMs make convenient: **Drizzle, Prisma** (Node.js), **GORM** (Go), **SQLAlchemy** (Python). The ORM abstracts the database, and unless you write raw SQL, you can switch between Postgres, SQLite, and MySQL.

But the behaviors differ. For example, in **Postgres**, `LIKE` is **case-sensitive** and `ILIKE` is the case-insensitive version. In **SQLite**, `LIKE` is **case-insensitive by default for ASCII**. A search feature built and tested locally on SQLite will quietly behave differently in production on Postgres, **with no error, no warning, and no log**, because your assumption was wrong.

The document’s advice: use the **same backing services** (database, cache, queue) in local, staging, and production. Today that is hardly an excuse, since starting a container takes one command, nothing like 2011.

### But in 2026 it’s less clean

We started with Docker and Docker Compose, where local and production used the same containers. Then we moved to **managed services**. If production uses **AWS DynamoDB**, you cannot run DynamoDB locally. Emulators such as **LocalStack** emulate many AWS services, but DynamoDB is proprietary and what we know of its behavior comes from documentation, so it is not the same implementation. This creates a gap, and there is no simple rule such as “avoid managed services”, because they bring large benefits: easier scaling and lower cost than running your own infrastructure.

**Nuanced takeaway:** if the benefit of a managed service is large, it is worth accepting the trade-off of two distinct environments.

## Factor XI: Logs

> *Treat logs as event streams.*

Because of history, we think of a log as a **file**. This factor says the file was never the log. A file is just one possible output format. The log itself is a **stream of events ordered by time**, flowing as long as the application runs, with no beginning and no end.

Once you think of logs as a stream, the factor’s intuition follows: your app should **not be involved in routing or storing that stream**. It should not care about log file paths or **log rotation**. What it prescribes:

- the app writes **every event to standard output** (what you see in the terminal when you run it), **unbuffered**;
- the **execution environment** (platform, orchestrator) **captures** the stream and decides where it goes: a file, or a **log drain** (a backing service) for analysis or storage.

### Why divide it this way?

1. Your app doesn’t worry about logging needs per deployment. **No code changes** when you deploy to a different environment, because it is now a **config/platform** matter. In 2011, standard output typically fed **syslog**. Today it feeds something like **Loki** or **Datadog**. The pattern is identical.
2. **A stream composes with other things, a file does not.** In the three environments:
   - on your **laptop**: logs print live in the terminal;
   - in a **container**: the runtime captures the stream and stamps each line with which process produced it, then hands it to a collector;
   - in **production**: the platform takes the streams from all instances and **merges them into one time-ordered stream**, so you care about *when* each event happened rather than which instance it came from. The overhead of understanding, analyzing, and searching logs across many instances largely disappears.

### What aged

We now have the whole field of **observability**: logs, **metrics**, and **traces**. The 2011 text mentions only logs, since metrics and traces were not common practice then. Treat these as **additions**, not as limits the factor places on you.

---

## Factor XII: Admin Processes

> *Run admin and management tasks as one-off processes.*

A **one-off process** is a task you run once, next to your long-running application process, and then it exits. Picture an HTTP backend serving requests that also needs to **run a database migration** or **a script to fix some data in production**.

The factor says where such tasks should run: a one-off admin process must run in an **identical environment** to the app’s regular processes: the **same codebase at the same commit** (so one deployment carries both), the **same config**, and the **same dependency isolation model**.

### Why so strict?

In the old days people **SSH’d into the running server** and ran an update or delete query by hand, with **no code review, no process review, and no record** of what was done in production. That produced drift between production and what exists in code or documentation. The most famous example of this factor done right is the **database migration**: it ships with the same code and runs as a one-off process **before the app starts**, then exits.

## All Twelve in Practice: The Tasker Demo

To see the factors working together, the series’ **Tasker** backend runs on a Linux box reached over SSH, driven by a **Procfile** (a file invented by Heroku to declare process types: the web process, the worker process, the scheduler, and so on).

- A process manager called **Overmind** starts the whole **formation**.
- During boot you can see, in order: the **schema check**, then the **background job server**, then the **server binding itself to its assigned port**. This is the sequence discussed across the factors.
- The app’s **log stream** goes to standard output.
- In a second pane, a **one-off admin process** (auto-archiving) runs from the **same code, same config, same environment**, then exits.
- On **Ctrl-C**, the shutdown is clean: the **connection pool closes**, the **job server waits for its workers to finish their jobs**, and the process reports a clean exit. This demonstrates factors **VIII, IX, and XII**.

## Summary: The Twelve Factors at a Glance

| # | Factor | One-line meaning | How it aged |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | **Codebase** | One codebase in version control, many deploys | Obvious now. Monorepos are fine: one app per codebase. The real violation is copy-paste sharing. |
| II | **Dependencies** | Explicitly declare and isolate | Lock files strengthened it. Containers practically solved isolation. |
| III | **Config** | Store config in the environment | Separation from code is non-negotiable. Secrets increasingly belong in a secret manager. |
| IV | **Backing services** | Treat them as attached resources | Aged very well. It predicted managed databases, caches, and email. |
| V | **Build, release, run** | Strictly separate the stages | Immutability is now explicit. Rollbacks in seconds. |
| VI | **Processes** | Stateless, share-nothing | The default everywhere. Break it deliberately when the feature itself is state (WebSockets). |
| VII | **Port binding** | Export services via a port | Serverless adds a second shape: a declared interface. |
| VIII | **Concurrency** | Scale out via the process model | Adopted by orchestrators so deeply we rarely notice. |
| IX | **Disposability** | Fast startup, graceful shutdown | Even more relevant: platforms constantly start and stop processes. |
| X | **Dev/prod parity** | Keep environments similar | Partly eroded by managed services with no local equivalent. |
| XI | **Logs** | Treat logs as event streams | Still core. Metrics and traces are additions. |
| XII | **Admin processes** | Run one-off tasks in the same environment | Migrations are the classic example. |

**The big picture:** the twelve-factor app is a contract between your application and any platform. Follow it and your app can be run, scaled, and updated by any machine, platform, or new teammate **without surprises**, and you are protected from the slow decay that the document calls **software erosion**.
