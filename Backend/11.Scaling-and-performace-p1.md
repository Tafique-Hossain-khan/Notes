# Thinking Clearly About Backend Performance and Scaling

*Latency, percentiles, throughput, utilization, bottlenecks, profiling, tracing, and the database problems that cause most slowdowns*

---

## Introduction

"Scaling" and "performance" are two of the most widely used buzzwords in engineering, and they are also two of the most important concepts when we talk about systems, backends, and infrastructure. The topic spreads across many domains. Performance means something different for browser systems and front ends than it does for infrastructure. This post narrows the discussion to what matters in **backend engineering**.

We start from the primary question, *what do we even mean by performance?*, and build up layer by layer. The goal is an intuition for:

- how systems behave under load,
- where bottlenecks actually hide, and
- how to think clearly when everything seems to be falling apart under load.

The aim is not to memorize a handful of scaling techniques. It is to learn a way of thinking about scaling and performance that applies to any system you meet in the future, backend or otherwise. Before reaching for terms like vertical scaling and horizontal scaling, we build the mental models so you can connect the dots yourself.

---

## 1. What Is Latency?

What do we mean when we say a system is "fast"? Consider a modern web app. A user clicks a button, and a series of events starts:

1. The browser (say Chrome) sends a request across the internet to our server, the server that backs this web app.
2. The server receives and processes the request.
3. If needed, the server talks to the **database**, either to store information or to fetch something the user needs.
4. If needed, the server also calls **other APIs**. For example, to send an email it might call an email service such as Resend.
5. The server sends a response, typically JSON, which travels back across the internet to the browser.
6. The browser parses the JSON, runs some JavaScript, and renders the result on screen, say a couple of cards.

Some amount of time passes between the click and the cards appearing. That whole interval is **latency**.

Latency is one of the most fundamental concepts in performance. To talk about performance we need a measurable unit, a number, instead of figurative words like "fast" or "slow". Latency is also what users actually *feel*. When someone says "your app is slow", they are talking about latency, whether or not they know the word.

**Latency is not a single number.** It is not as simple as saying "500 milliseconds". It varies from request to request. One request might finish in 50 ms and another might take 200 ms. In the real world, nothing is as predictable as it is on a whiteboard. Why might two requests differ?

- One request hit a **cache**, either a CDN or an in-memory cache like Redis. Its result was already sitting in storage that is much faster, and closer to the user, than the database. The other request had to travel all the way to the server and run a database query, so it took about 200 ms.
- One request arrived when the server was **idle**. The other arrived when the server was already processing around 50 other requests concurrently.

Any of these can be the reason, because we are dealing with real-world traffic.

---

## 2. Why Averages Are Misleading

Suppose two requests took 50 ms and 200 ms. The average is 125 ms. Does that number tell you anything meaningful about your system? Averages are useful statistics in many contexts, but for performance they can be very misleading, and are arguably close to useless, because **the variation is what matters**, and an average hides it.

Here is a concrete scenario. You measure a thousand requests and compute an average latency of about 100 ms. That looks healthy. But hidden inside it is this: 99% of requests completed in under 50 ms, while the other 1% took around 5 seconds.

That 1% represents real users having a terrible experience. Scale the math up: if your backend serves **1 million requests per day**, then **10,000 requests** per day waited 5 seconds. After clicking a button or landing on a screen, those users stared at a loader for 5 seconds before any data appeared. The average says everything is fine. You never see the frustration, and you never discover your system's limits at the tail.

That is why, when we talk about performance, we talk about **percentiles**, not averages.

---

## 3. Percentiles: P50, P90, P99

Before you understand the mechanics of a system, you need to understand the lingo of its ecosystem, so that you can follow the different opinions spread across it. Percentiles are a core part of the lingo of performance.

- **P50 (50th percentile, the median).** If P50 is 400 ms, then half of your requests complete in 400 ms or less. The other half take longer.
- **P90.** If P90 is 900 ms, then 90% of requests finish in 900 ms or less, and **10%** of users experience more than 900 ms.
- **P99.** If P99 is 2 seconds, then 99% of requests finish in under 2 seconds, and **1%** of users experience 2 seconds or more.

A useful way to think about it: subtract the percentile from 100. For P99, that is 1% of users on the slow side. For P90, that is 10%.

These three numbers (P50, P90, P99) come up every time you discuss latency, performance, optimization, and scaling. If you picture a latency distribution as a graph, most users sit in the fast part, a smaller group sits around P90, and a thin tail near P99 experiences the maximum latency.

### Why backend engineers obsess over P95 and P99

In practice, backend engineers focus heavily on **P95 and P99**. It is not only about caring for unhappy users. The requests that land in the tail are often the ones that do the most complex work:

- the most complex business logic,
- the most complex database queries, and
- the most complex service coordination, such as calling external services, sending emails, and waiting for webhook calls.

The users who hit those paths often represent your **most valuable customers**. A user making a payment or a purchase triggers more complex queries and logic than a user casually browsing. Caring about P99 and P95 is therefore also about caring about the workflows that matter most to the business.

---

## 4. Throughput

Latency tells us how long **one request** takes from origin to response. **Throughput** tells us how many requests the system can handle in a given period, typically measured in **requests per second** or **requests per minute**.

Throughput matters because latency alone can look great in isolation. At 10 requests per second your latency might be a comfortable 150 ms. But when load rises to 1,000 requests per second, latency can jump to 2 seconds. So you need to read the two together.

Understanding throughput alongside latency lets you answer practical questions:

- Can our system, whether an e-commerce site, a learning platform, or a course seller, survive the **Black Friday** rush, when traffic spikes from normal levels to hundreds of thousands or millions of requests?
- Can it survive a sudden **email campaign**, or being featured on a popular **podcast, website, or blog**?
- How many **concurrent users** can we support?
- How many **requests per second** can we handle before we need more resources?

Latency and throughput are connected, but not in an intuitive way. As throughput increases, latency also increases, slowly at first, and then **dramatically** after some point. That relationship is a central idea in performance engineering, and the next section builds the intuition for it.

---

## 5. Utilization and the Latency Curve

### The ice cream shop

Imagine an ice cream shop with one worker serving customers.

**Sunday evening.** The shop is empty. You walk in, ask for an ice cream, pay, and get it immediately. This is **low utilization with low latency**. The worker is not working very hard (low utilization), and your request is served instantly (low latency).

**Tuesday lunchtime.** Now there is a huge rush and you are standing in a long line. The worker has not slowed down at all. Each ice cream still takes 2 minutes. The worker's execution rate is exactly the same. But *your perceived wait time has grown enormously*, because you have to wait for everyone ahead of you.

Backend servers behave the same way. When utilization of CPU, memory, and other resources is low, a request arrives and a response goes out almost immediately. As more requests arrive, each one waits for the one ahead of it, and **a queue forms**. The higher the utilization, the longer the queue, and the longer each request waits.

### What utilization means

**Utilization** is the percentage of your system's capacity that is currently in use. At 0% the system is idle. At 100% it is maxed out, can do nothing more, and is at the brink of collapse.

### The counterintuitive curve

Plot utilization on the x-axis and latency on the y-axis. Intuition says latency should grow **linearly** with utilization. It does not. Latency stays fairly flat for a long time, and as utilization approaches 100% the curve bends upward and grows **exponentially**. Wrapping your head around this curve is essential to understanding performance and scaling.

### The highway analogy

Think of a national highway:

- **50% capacity.** There is room for cars to overtake and change lanes. Traffic flows smoothly, speeds are maintained, and everyone arrives in a predictable time.
- **80% capacity.** Slowdowns appear. Overtaking and lane changes are harder, and you have to think twice before changing lanes.
- **90% capacity.** Traffic becomes **unpredictable**. Sometimes it flows when the gaps happen to line up. Sometimes it jams, especially with impatient drivers, because everyone wants to pass and nobody gives way. Every brake, lane change, or merge creates a ripple effect, and everyone behind starts honking and cannot move.
- **100% capacity.** There is a car in every spot. Nobody can move. The whole road is blocked.

### Headroom and bursty traffic

The lesson is that **you cannot run a system at 100% utilization and expect it to perform**. You need headroom, spare capacity that can absorb traffic spikes. Real production systems usually run at around **60 to 80% utilization**, keeping roughly 20% as a buffer.

There is a second reason for the buffer: traffic is not smooth. It is not a metronome delivering a fixed number of requests every second. Traffic comes in **bursts**. One minute a lot of requests show up, the next minute there are none, and half an hour later a thousand arrive at once. Even if your *average* traffic sits well below capacity, at 40 or 50%, a burst can instantly push you above 100%. Without headroom, the system crashes.

So remember three things: understand utilization, understand its exponential relationship with latency, and never run at 100%. This is a mental model we will keep connecting back to.

## 6. Finding Bottlenecks: Measure, Don't Guess

When we say a system is slow, something *specific* in it is causing the slowness. Finding that specific thing is called **identifying the bottleneck**.

This sounds obvious, but in practice we often skip it and jump straight to the standard by-the-book fixes:

- "Add caching", because we have heard that caching is the answer to every latency problem.
- "The database is the problem, so upgrade it", for instance Postgres 16 to Postgres 18, or just upgrade some packages.
- "The server is the bottleneck, so add more servers", which is **horizontal scaling**.

Sometimes we get lucky. More often we spend days or weeks implementing a solution to a problem we do not have, while the real bottleneck stays untouched and the system stays slow.

### A worked example

Suppose we have a `GET /products/:id` endpoint that returns the details of a product, and it appears slow. The first instinct, and honestly the first instinct of most of us, is that the database must be the problem, since it always is. So we add Redis in front of the database and spend a week building and deploying a caching layer.

After deployment, the API is **still slow**. So we finally do what we should have done first: add granular timing measurements throughout the code. When does the request arrive? When is the database query called? When is the cache called? What do we find?

- The database query takes about **10 ms**.
- The new Redis cache takes about **5 ms**.
- A **logging function** in the same service writes synchronously to a remote logging service (Elasticsearch or similar). Remote logging is normal and not a problem in itself, but here the request was **blocked** while the log call was made, and the whole thing took about **500 ms**.

The database was never the problem. Without measuring, we followed the book, added caching, and missed the real culprit: the *synchronous* nature of the logging call. (Concurrency and parallelism, the contrast between blocking and doing work alongside the request, are covered in depth in the next video of this series.)

The same pattern repeats. The real cause might be a JSON or XML serialization step. It might be that the code is fast but the **response payload is huge**, so network transmission dominates. Instead of measuring each part of the workflow, we blame the database, the indexing strategy, or the caching strategy.

> **The lesson: never guess, always measure.** Measurement is the foundation of performance, scaling, and optimization work.

## 7. Profiling and Distributed Tracing

### Profiling

**Profiling** is the practice of measuring where your application actually spends its time. A profiler attaches to your running application and, during real request processing, records samples: which functions are executing, when they started, when they returned, and so on, end to end.

Profiler output can be overwhelming, with thousands of functions each accounting for a fraction of total time. That is why we use **flame graphs**. A flame graph shows the **call stack over time** (the function execution chain):

- Functions that take **more time appear wider**, so they draw your attention.
- Functions called by other functions are **stacked on top** of their callers.

Just by looking at a flame graph, without reading raw output, you can tell where the application spends most of its time. The first time you profile a real application you will probably be surprised, because the bottleneck is rarely where you expect. You assume it is complex business logic (CPU-bound work), but the time goes into serializing JSON. You expect slow database queries, but the culprit is an external API call made inside a loop.

### CPU-bound vs. I/O-bound

In practice, profilers are better at **CPU-bound** work, where the application is actually burning CPU cycles on computation. They are less effective for **I/O-bound** work, which means anything that deals with input/output and waiting for results: database queries, file handling, serialization, external API calls, queues.

Most backend performance problems in typical SaaS applications are **I/O-bound**, whether at the database, an external service, or a queue. The exceptions are domains like image processing or machine learning, which involve heavy computation and matrix manipulation. Since CPU profilers are weak at catching I/O waits, we need another tool.

### Distributed tracing

**Distributed tracing**, which falls under **observability**, follows a single request as it flows through your system. It records when the request entered each component, when it started a database query, when it entered and left a function, when it began an external API call, and so on.

Once tracing is set up, you might see that a request to `/products/5` spent only **2 ms** in your actual business logic but **800 ms** in the database query. Now you know exactly where to focus. Tracing is a very fast way to find where latency lives in an API.

## 8. Database Bottlenecks

Databases are typically the bottleneck in most backend applications, at least when we try to optimize performance, and there are good reasons we look at them first. Databases do the hard work:

- They store data **durably** on disk so it survives restarts.
- They handle inserts, updates, and deletes while guarding against crashes and failures.
- They provide **consistency guarantees** by handling concurrent reads and writes and locking, backed by a great deal of ongoing research.
- They execute **complex queries** across millions or billions of rows.

All of this takes time, so we cannot blame databases blindly. But there are common low-hanging fruits. The two big ones are the **N+1 query problem** and **missing indexes**.

---

## 9. The N+1 Query Problem

### Starting from the front end

Suppose you are building a React blog app. The home page shows a list of 20 posts, and each post must display its **author's name**. The list API does not return author details. So the client makes **one** call to fetch the 20 posts, then loops over them and makes **another** call per post to fetch the author. That is 1 + 20 = **21 API calls** for a single page.

This is the **N+1 query pattern**: one query to fetch N items, then N queries to fetch one detail for each.

- 20 items → 21 calls
- 100 items → 101 calls
- 1,000 items → 1,001 calls

The number of calls grows **linearly** with the number of items shown. Any engineer senses something is wrong: we should not make a thousand requests to show a thousand items.

### The hidden cost of each query

Even setting aside that obvious smell, every query carries overhead:

- The network trip between application server and database, in both directions.
- A **TCP connection setup** if you do not use connection pooling.
- The database must **parse** the query, **plan** its execution, **execute** it, and **return** the results.

Even if one query takes an excellent 5 ms, 1,000 queries take **5,000 ms, which is 5 seconds**. Users staring at a loader for 5 seconds is not acceptable by 2025 standards.

### The fix: fetch in bulk

Instead of 1,000 author queries, collect all the author IDs into a single list and fetch them in **one** query. Now it is **two queries in total**: one for the posts and one for their authors. It does not matter whether you show 20, 100, or 1,000 posts. It is always two queries.

### It really happens at the server

The front-end framing was just for intuition. The server does not store data itself. It fetches posts and authors from the database. So the real N+1 problem is at the **server level**: your backend is effectively the "front end" of your database. The classic ORM version looks like this:

```
posts = db.select(posts).where(user_id = ...)
for post in posts:
    author = db.select(users).where(id = post.author_id)
```

This looks like perfectly normal code, and that is exactly the trap. ORMs let you access data using your language's types and constructs, so it feels familiar, and we stop thinking about the SQL the ORM generates behind the scenes.

### The tools your ORM gives you

Most modern ORMs have primitives to bulk-fetch related data, so you can tell the ORM up front to load what you need:

- **Django:** `select_related` for foreign-key relationships and `prefetch_related` for many-to-many style relationships.
- **Ruby on Rails:** `includes`.
- **TypeScript ORMs (e.g. TypeORM):** `leftJoin` and `select`.
- **Prisma, Drizzle, and others:** equivalent bulk-fetch or join primitives.
- **Raw SQL:** use `JOIN`s (inner or left).

Result: one or two SQL queries instead of a thousand. Modern ORMs also let you **print the actual SQL** they run. Turn this on during development to confirm the query pattern is what you intend, or to decide whether to write raw SQL yourself.

**Takeaway:** whenever you use an ORM and a database, watch for N+1, and use joins or your ORM's bulk-fetch constructs to avoid it.

## 10. Database Indexes

The most common source of database performance problems is **indexes**, or more precisely, the **absence** of them.

### The library analogy

Imagine a library with a million books spread across floors, sections, rooms, and shelves, but **no catalog**. A reader asks for every book by John Green. With no catalog, you must walk through the entire library, examining every book, and collect the matches. That might take three days. If another reader asks the same thing, or the same reader comes back, you start the whole three-day journey again, because the books went back onto the shelves in random order.

This is a **sequential scan**, also called a **full table scan**: reading every row of a table to find the ones you want. In a real database it does not take three days, but on a very large table it can take one to two seconds, which is still slow by modern standards.

Now suppose you keep a **catalog organized by author name**, listing for each author the exact locations of their books. You look up John Green and go directly to the right shelves, which takes two to three minutes instead of three days.

### What an index really is

In a database, an index is a data structure, usually a **B-tree** (there are other kinds, such as hash indexes, but B-tree is what typically serves this use case). It maintains a **sorted copy of a column's values**, each with pointers to the matching rows. If you index a `posts` or `books` table by `author_id`, the database keeps a sorted list of author IDs with pointers to rows. To find all books by author 3, it follows the pointers directly instead of scanning every row.

The difference can be dramatic. On a table with a million rows, a lookup that takes around **4 seconds** with a sequential scan can drop to roughly **40 ms**, or at least under 100 ms, with an index.

### Indexes are not free

Indexes are not a one-stop fix for database performance. They have costs:

1. **Storage.** Each index stores its sorted structure and pointers somewhere, and its size grows with the table.
2. **Write overhead.** Every insert, update, or delete on the table must also update every index on it, so the index stays in sync and `SELECT` queries stay correct. If you index every column, your **write operations slow down significantly** because each write updates all those index structures one by one.

### Deciding what to index

- In Postgres, the **primary key (ID) is already indexed**, so you do not need to index it explicitly. Searching by ID uses that index.
- Other columns, such as `author_id` on a books table, you must index yourself. If the access pattern is obvious (searching books by author is such a common use case), add the index when you define the table, before you even write the APIs.
- Remember that **joins also use indexes**.
- For non-obvious columns, **wait**. Monitor the app, look at logs and traces, and let data tell you. For example, distributed tracing might show that a frequently hit endpoint is slow because a query filters on the **ISBN** column, which has no index. *That* is when you add the ISBN index.

Because indexes carry real overhead, be cautious and not generous with them.

### Composite indexes

A **composite index** covers multiple columns. If you frequently query by both `user_id` and `created_at`, a composite index on `(user_id, created_at)` serves that query better than two separate single-column indexes.

**Column order matters.** With the order `(user_id, created_at)`:

- Queries using **both** columns can use the index.
- Queries using **only `user_id`** can still use it, since it is the leading column.
- Queries using **only `created_at`** will **not** use it.

### Covering indexes

A **covering index** contains all the columns a query needs, so the database can answer entirely from the index without going back to the table. For example, take a `departments` table with 100 columns where the front end only ever needs the department `name` and `id`. An index that includes the name (with the ID available) lets a name-only query be served directly from the index, never touching the table's contents. You can include more columns, but doing so makes the index larger, so analyze whether the trade-off is worth it.

### How do you know what to index? `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`

Suppose tracing points you at a complex query with joins, grouping, and `HAVING`. How do you know which column needs an index? Most databases provide **`EXPLAIN ANALYZE`**. Put it in front of your query and the database shows you the full **query plan**:

- which table it started from,
- which tables it joined,
- which index (if any) it used to fetch each piece of data, and
- how the whole plan was executed from start to finish.

Read the plan to spot **sequential (full table) scans**, places where no index is used. If those tables have thousands or millions of rows, that scan is likely your latency source. Add an index there, then run `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` a second time to confirm the database now actually uses the index.

## Key Takeaways

| Concept | What to remember |
| --- | --- |
| **Latency** | The total time from user action to result. It is not one number. It varies per request. |
| **Averages** | Misleading for performance. 99% fast and 1% at 5 s can still average about 100 ms. |
| **Percentiles** | Use P50, P90, P99 (and P95). P99 means 1% of users are slower than that number. |
| **Why P99 matters** | The tail often contains your most complex, most valuable workflows. |
| **Throughput** | Requests per second or minute. Read it together with latency. |
| **Utilization** | Latency rises exponentially as utilization nears 100%. Run at roughly 60 to 80%. |
| **Burstiness** | Real traffic comes in bursts, so keep headroom even if average load is low. |
| **Bottlenecks** | Find the specific slow component. Never guess, always measure. |
| **Profiling** | Great for CPU-bound work. Flame graphs show where time goes. |
| **Distributed tracing** | Best for I/O-bound problems, which are the majority in SaaS backends. |
| **N+1 queries** | Fetch related data in bulk with joins or ORM prefetching. |
| **Indexes** | Turn full table scans into fast lookups. They cost storage and slow writes. |
| **Composite and covering indexes** | Column order matters. Covering indexes avoid table lookups. |
| **`EXPLAIN ANALYZE`** | Shows the query plan so you can see which scans lack an index. |

With this foundation, the vocabulary of performance and scaling is no longer a list of buzzwords. When you later meet vertical scaling, horizontal scaling, caching, and concurrency, you will know *which problem each one solves* and, just as importantly, how to measure whether it is the problem you actually have.

## 11. Closing the Loop on Indexes

Part 1 ended with `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`. Here is how the workflow completes. After you add an index, run the same `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` query a second time. If the database is using your new index, the plan no longer shows a **sequential scan** (full table scan). It shows an **index scan**, and the query should feel much faster.

The combined technique for database performance work is therefore:

1. Use **distributed tracing** to find the exact database query that is slow.
2. Use **`EXPLAIN ANALYZE`** to find the exact columns that lack useful indexes.
3. Add the index, then re-run `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` to confirm the plan changed.

That covers indexes. Next comes another database overhead that surprises people as they scale: the cost of connections.

---

## 12. Connection Pooling (Internal vs. External)

One realization that arrives once you start scaling a backend is that **database connections are not as cheap as you thought**. On a local machine they are so cheap that you never think about them or their effect on performance. At scale, they become expensive.

### What happens when your server connects to the database

When your application connects to a database, a chain of steps runs one after another:

1. A **TCP connection** is established through a three-way handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK). The network-engineering details are beyond our scope here, but the handshake costs a round trip.
2. **Authentication** is performed, if required.
3. **Encryption** is negotiated, including the key exchange infrastructure (public and private keys).
4. **Session state** is set up for data access.
5. The database **allocates memory** for the connection, a few megabytes to start with.

All of this takes time and resources every time a new connection is created.

### Two problems with a connection per operation

If your backend opens a new connection for every database operation and closes it immediately afterwards, two problems appear:

- **Latency.** You pay the full connection setup cost again and again, on every HTTP request and every query.
- **Connection limits.** A database caps the number of simultaneous connections. A typical Postgres instance allows somewhere around a few hundred, for example 400 to 600 depending on how much memory and CPU you configured. During a traffic spike or a busy sale day, your backend handles hundreds, thousands, or tens of thousands of concurrent requests and can easily **exhaust the connection limit**, and the database can fall over.

### The solution: a connection pool

**Connection pooling** solves both problems. Instead of your server talking to the database directly and opening a fresh connection each time, it talks to a middleman called the **pool**. The pool maintains a set of already-open, idle connections that are ready to use.

The flow looks like this:

1. Your server **borrows** a connection from the pool.
2. It runs its query on that connection.
3. It **returns** the connection to the pool.
4. The pool keeps the connection open, for a duration that depends on configuration, so the next query **reuses** it instead of creating a new one.

This removes both the repeated setup cost and the risk of an unbounded number of connections.

### Internal pooling

There are two pooling patterns. **Internal pooling** is what most modern database drivers give you out of the box: the driver itself maintains a pool of connections inside your application process. It is called internal because the pool is specific to that one server.

The catch appears when you scale horizontally. Modern applications do not run on a single server. When traffic spikes you add more instances. Each instance has its **own** pool.

Consider this scenario:

- Each server is configured with a pool of **150 connections**.
- A traffic spike triggers the Kubernetes autoscaler, and you now have **3 instances**.
- The maximum number of connections your fleet can open is 3 × 150 = **450**.
- But the database can only handle **300** connections.

When traffic spikes again and the instances together push to, say, 400 connections, the database exhausts its limit and crashes. Each pool was sensible on its own, but nothing coordinates them.

### External pooling

The fix is an **external pooler**. For Postgres, a very popular one is **PgBouncer**, a lightweight, open-source connection pooler.

Replay the same scenario. The autoscaler adds two more instances, but this time none of the servers holds its own pool. Instead, you run PgBouncer with a single pool of, say, 250 to 300 connections, and **all** server instances talk to PgBouncer over the network. PgBouncer in turn talks to the database. Because one pool sits in front of the database and serves every instance, a traffic spike cannot silently push the total beyond the database's limit.

When scaling toward production, and especially when you know your traffic spike patterns, an **external pooler such as PgBouncer is usually preferred over internal pooling**.

### Database concerns, recapped

We have now covered three database performance concerns:

1. The **N+1 query problem**.
2. **Indexing strategy**: what to do and what not to do.
3. The **overhead of connections**, and why pooling matters.

Suppose you have done all of this: queries are optimized, indexes are in place, and the connection pool is configured. The database is *still* the bottleneck and you still see latency at that layer. The obvious next step is **caching**.

## 13. Caching Strategies and Invalidation

(Caching has its own dedicated video in this series, so we will not go deep into mechanics. But a discussion of performance and scaling cannot skip it.)

The idea is beautifully simple: **store the result of an expensive operation**. In our case the expensive operation is the database layer, say a complex query. You store its result in Redis or another caching solution. The next time a request arrives, you take a detour: check the cache first. If the result is there, return it immediately. For example, if the full path takes around 500 to 800 ms and the cached path takes around 50 ms, you cut perceived latency from 800 ms to 50 ms in one move.

Every engineer knows that use case. The real difficulty lies elsewhere.

### Cache invalidation

A famous quote says there are only two hard problems in programming: **naming things** and **cache invalidation**. It is not an exaggeration. If you have implemented multi-layer caching, you know invalidation touches many layers: your servers, CDNs, clients' browsers, reverse proxies, and everything in between. When the underlying data, for example the result of that complex SQL query, changes, you cannot keep serving the old cached copy. You have to invalidate the cache and keep it in sync with the source of truth in the database.

There are two main techniques.

**Time-based expiration (TTL).** You decide in advance how long an entry stays valid, say 5 or 10 minutes. After that it expires, and the next request consults the original data source. It is simple, but picking the optimal duration is hard. It depends on user access patterns and how often the API is hit, and that differs from endpoint to endpoint and service to service.

**Event-based invalidation.** Whenever the original data changes, you invalidate the cache entry. For example, you cache a user's profile. In the same handler that updates the name, email, or preferences, right after updating the database, you **delete the cache entry**. The next `GET` for that profile finds no cache entry, reads fresh data from the database, and returns it. You no longer guess a TTL, because you know exactly when to invalidate. The downside is discipline: at *every* place in code that updates the profile you must remember to invalidate. Miss even one, and there is no automatic expiry to save you, so you risk serving **stale data**.

Both approaches have pros and cons.

### Where does the cache live? Local vs. distributed

**Local caching** means storing cached values in a data structure such as a dictionary or map in your own server's memory. The problem: with 10 server instances, each has its own copy of the cache, so you get **cache inconsistency** across servers.

**Distributed caching** uses an external service such as **Redis, Memcached, or Valkey** (a modern option). All 10 instances talk to the same cache, so inconsistency disappears. The trade-off is that each cache read needs a **network round trip**. A local in-memory lookup is nearly instant. The speaker cites a few milliseconds for local access versus around 50 ms once a network hop is involved, even on an internal network. The exact numbers vary with payload size and serialization format, but the direction is clear: the network costs time.

### Tiered caching

Many systems combine both with **tiered caching**: a small **local cache** holds the *hottest* data (the definition of "hot" depends on your eviction algorithm, for example most recently used or most frequently used), backed by a **distributed cache** like Redis. A lookup first checks the local cache, then the distributed cache. After fetching from the distributed cache, the system can decide, based on how hot the item is, whether to also store it locally.

### Caching patterns

A caching pattern defines **when** data gets cached and how reads and writes interact with the cache. Which one you choose depends on the access pattern of your application, your users, and the specific endpoint. There are three primary patterns.

**1. Cache-aside (lazy loading).** The most widely used pattern, and one you have probably implemented without knowing its name. For `GET /users/2`:

- Check the cache. If the profile is present, return it immediately.
- If not, query the database, run the complex query, **save the result in the cache**, then return the response.
- On an update to that user, application code explicitly **deletes (invalidates)** the cache entry, so the next read goes to the database and fetches fresh data. You never serve stale data.

**2. Write-through.** On every write, the system updates **both the database and the cache** at the same time, instead of just invalidating.

- *Advantage:* there is never a cache miss. The entry is always present.
- *Disadvantage:* writes take a few more milliseconds, because both the database and the cache must be written before returning success.

**3. Write-behind (write-back).** The system updates the **cache first**, which is extremely fast, returns success, and then updates the database **asynchronously**.

- *Advantage:* it avoids the latency of writing both layers synchronously.
- *Disadvantage:* if the asynchronous database write fails, the cache and database end up **inconsistent**.

### Cache hit ratio

The **cache hit ratio** is the percentage of requests that get their answer from the cache instead of the original source. A 90% hit ratio means 90% of requests are served from the cache and only 10% reach the database. That is very good. A ratio like 20% means that even though you built a caching layer, something is wrong with your algorithm or pattern and you need to find and fix it.

Three factors strongly affect hit ratio:

- **TTL.** A longer TTL keeps entries around longer, so there is a better chance of future hits. But a very long TTL, say 7 days, increases staleness and defeats the purpose of having a TTL, and then you must actively fight stale data.
- **Cache size.** More memory means more entries held and more hits, which is fairly obvious.
- **Data access patterns.** If you do not know which endpoints are hit frequently, and when, in other words if you do not understand your users' behavior, your strategy will not work and you will not reach hit ratios like 70 to 80% or higher. The hit ratio depends directly on how well you understand how your users use the platform.

## 14. Scaling Directly: Vertical vs. Horizontal

We have talked about the factors that affect scaling. Now we turn to scaling itself, the textbook answers. Your application runs on servers. As traffic and the user base grow, those servers' resources become insufficient, and over time you need more and more capacity. How do you get it? There are two fundamental approaches: **vertical scaling** and **horizontal scaling**.

---

## 15. Vertical Scaling (Scaling Up)

**Vertical scaling**, or **scaling up**, means making a single server more powerful. Picture increasing the "height" of your server: you replace the old machine with a bigger one. This is done through hardware upgrades:

- **CPU:** add more cores (2, 4, 8, and so on).
- **Memory (RAM):** the fastest primary memory. Go from 2 GB to 4, 8, 16, 32 GB, and beyond, as your needs dictate.
- **Storage:** more capacity (30 GB to 300 GB to 1 TB), and faster media such as **SSDs and NVMe SSDs**, which offer far faster access.
- **Network card:** modern cards at 10 Gbps and above can handle more internet traffic.

### The appeal

The beauty of vertical scaling is **simplicity and intuitiveness**:

- Your **architecture does not change**. You do not need to think about statelessness or other code-level changes. Just upgrade the machine, and your code stays the same.
- Capacity scales predictably. A server with twice the CPUs can handle roughly twice the requests. A server with twice the RAM can cache twice as much data locally. Go from 16 GB to 32 GB and your caching capacity doubles.
- It is often **cheaper**: one server with twice the power typically costs less than two servers with half the size.
- You avoid the **operational overhead** of managing multiple machines: security, backups, maintenance, load balancers, and distributed state.

For many applications, vertical scaling makes a lot of sense.

### The limits

If it is so simple, why doesn't everyone do it? Because vertical scaling has hard limits:

1. **A hard ceiling.** No matter how much money you spend, there is a limit on how powerful one machine can be. Cloud providers such as GCP, DigitalOcean, or AWS offer large instances, but eventually you reach the biggest machine they offer. If you still need more capacity, you cannot scale vertically any further.
2. **Single point of failure.** Picture a beast of a machine with 32 cores and 96 GB of RAM. Servers crash every day for many reasons, such as a dependency problem. If it goes down for 2 hours, your platform is **completely unavailable** for those 2 hours. Standby servers and automatic failover can mitigate this, but a single server, however powerful, remains a single point of failure.
3. **No geographic distribution.** Suppose your big server sits in the USA but **40% of your users are in India**. That 40% experiences higher latency than the other 60%, and with one server you can do nothing about it.

Because of these drawbacks, most of the industry moved toward the other approach.

---

## 16. Horizontal Scaling (Scaling Out)

Instead of replacing a server with a more powerful one, **horizontal scaling** adds **more instances** of the same server so they work together to serve growing traffic. Rather than one powerful machine serving everyone, many medium-sized machines serve the user base.

### The advantages (theoretical ones, as we will revisit)

- **Linear capacity.** If one server handles 1,000 requests per second, five servers can in principle handle 5,000. Big campaign coming? Add more instances. Keep adding as needed.
- **No hard limit.** There is no cap on how many servers you can configure to work together.
- **Redundancy.** If one server fails, user traffic is redistributed across the remaining instances. Your service does not stop.
- **Geographic distribution.** You can place instances in different regions, and a **load balancer** can route each request to the nearest server based on the user's location, minimizing latency. That solves the problem vertical scaling could not.

### The disadvantages: complexity

If horizontal scaling had only pros, nobody would mention vertical scaling. Its cost is **complexity**, the same complexity vertical scaling avoids. The moment you add a second (or tenth) instance, questions appear:

- **How do you distribute requests across servers?** You need a **load balancer**, a brand new component in your infrastructure stack, and every new component adds complexity.
- **Which load-balancing algorithm** will you use? That is another decision to make.
- **How do you keep servers in sync?** If a user updates their name on server 1, how does server 2 find out?
- **What happens when the network connecting the servers fails?** How do they communicate, and what if they start making **redundant or conflicting decisions** because the communication medium is down?
- **What happens when one of three instances dies?** How do you detect that it is unavailable, decide when to stop routing requests to it, and decide when to start routing to it again once it comes back online?

All of these questions have answers, and the industry has worked them out. But the answers are complex and involve trade-offs, because we are now in the world of **distributed systems**. Distributed systems do not make problems disappear. They **transform one set of problems into a different set of problems** by making trade-offs. Sometimes the new set is more favorable than the original. Sometimes the original problems were more solvable than the new ones. Choosing between horizontal and vertical scaling comes down to deciding which set of problems you would rather deal with.

## Key Takeaways from Part 2

| Concept | What to remember |
| --- | --- |
| **Connection cost** | Each new DB connection involves TCP handshake, authentication, encryption negotiation, session setup, and memory allocation. |
| **Connection limits** | Databases cap connections (often a few hundred). Spikes can exhaust them. |
| **Connection pooling** | Borrow and return reusable connections instead of opening a new one per query. |
| **Internal pooling** | Per-server pool. With autoscaling, total connections can exceed the database limit (3 × 150 = 450 vs. 300). |
| **External pooling** | One shared pool (for example PgBouncer) in front of the database. Preferred at scale. |
| **Caching** | Can cut 800 ms to about 50 ms, but cache invalidation is the hard part. |
| **TTL vs. event-based invalidation** | TTL needs a well-chosen duration. Event-based needs discipline in every write path. |
| **Local vs. distributed cache** | Local is fast but inconsistent across instances. Distributed is consistent but costs a network hop. Tiered caching combines both. |
| **Caching patterns** | Cache-aside (most common), write-through (no misses, slower writes), write-behind (fast writes, risk of inconsistency). |
| **Cache hit ratio** | Driven by TTL, cache size, and how well you understand access patterns. About 90% is good. About 20% signals a problem. |
| **Vertical scaling** | Simple and no code changes, but has a hard ceiling, a single point of failure, and no geographic distribution. |
| **Horizontal scaling** | Linear capacity, no hard limit, redundancy, and geo-distribution, at the cost of load balancers, state sync, network failures, and health detection. |

The discussion of horizontal scaling stops at the point where the real questions begin: load balancing, state synchronization, and failure handling in distributed systems.
